Taxonomy meets Tech Community-centered

Photograph a plant so a model can learn from it

A model can only learn what it is shown. In Lebanon, a university team and a biosphere reserve built their own plant dataset with student volunteers, by turning a taxonomist's knowledge into steps anyone with a phone can follow.

Taxonomy meets Tech works through the Saving Lebanon's Biodiversity Center Incubator at the American University of Beirut, with the Shouf Biosphere Reserve. Its volunteers built FloraLebanon, a verified collection of photos of local plants. In five steps, learn how they photographed plants and how experts decided which photos the model would see.

Example data The plant drawings and 'submissions' below are examples we drew. They are not photos from FloraLebanon or screens from the team's app, and no species is named. Nothing is uploaded from this page.

Step one of fiveWhy the reserve needed its own photos

The reserve's management had many photos of plants nobody had identified yet. Even a full list of names would not have helped them decide how to protect the reserve.

'They said, we have all these images, if you want, we can give them to you on CDs and tell us what they are. Even if we identified all the plants, the information will remain a static list that can be posted on a website, but it is not functional.'

Taxonomy meets Tech, from our case study

International image collections did not fill the gap. Plants from Lebanon and the region were underrepresented, and the photos that existed showed plants from distances and angles that do not match how people see them on a walk through the reserve. A model trained only on those photos might do well in testing and then struggle in the field.

So the team decided to build the data with the community: students and local volunteers, with experts checking every picture.

Step two of fiveFour steps for every plant

Rather than asking every volunteer to become a taxonomist, the team turned expert knowledge into a few clear actions. In their app, each step is one short sentence and one illustration. Here is our version of the four steps.

Step three of fiveKeep or reject?

After every field trip, the team's experts went through the new photos and removed the blurred, repeated and poor ones before anything reached the model. Try it with these six example submissions.

    Step four of fiveFrom a photo to the dataset

    A photo that passes the check becomes part of the dataset, with what the app records alongside it.

    1. A volunteer takes the photos, following the protocol
    2. Experts check each one and keep or reject it
    3. The photo is labeled, with its location and season
    4. It joins the FloraLebanon dataset
    5. Later: identifying plants from a photo

    We stop here on purpose. When we spoke with the team, the identification feature was still being tested and not yet working, so this walk-through does not pretend to name a plant.

    What kept volunteers coming back was partly the field trips themselves and partly the app: points for each validated photo, and missions to villages and species with few pictures so far.

    A data question to think about. Every photo in a dataset like this carries a location. That is what makes it useful for conservation, and it is also a risk for a rare plant that someone might want to dig up. When should a dataset hide or blur where a plant grows, and who decides? This is our question for anyone building a similar project, not a description of the team's practice.

    Step five of fiveTry it near you

      The community-centered reading

      The decision that mattered most here was not which model to use but who builds the data and how. The team began by asking the reserve's staff which needs mattered most, then wrote a protocol simple enough for any volunteer and kept experts in charge of what counted as a good picture. The dataset is built from, and for, the place it describes.

      To think about: whose knowledge would your tool need, and how could you turn it into steps other people can follow?
