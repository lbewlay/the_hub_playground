Crop disease detection in Ghana Ecological

From photo to diagnosis, confidence and an expert

A diagnosis app gives a guess and says how sure it is. The most important design choice is what happens when the guess is shaky, and what happens when the climate changes under the model.

Farmers in Ghana told the University of Energy and Natural Resources that pests and diseases were hurting their crops. With them, the university built an app for basic Android phones that diagnoses a problem from a photo, works offline and recommends environmentally friendly remedies instead of chemical sprays. In five steps, follow one example photo through the app's logic.

Example data The leaf drawings, diagnoses, confidence levels and expert replies below are examples we wrote to show how confidence works. They are not output from the app, and nothing here is advice for a real plant.

Step 1 of 5Pick a photo

In the app, a farmer takes a picture of the affected part of the plant with the phone's camera, or picks one from the gallery. Choose one of these three example cashew leaves. You can come back and try the others.

Step 2 of 5The diagnosis, and how sure it is

The app names the likely problem and gives a confidence level: how sure the model is about its own guess. The team described it like this:

'It tells you this is probably say anthracnose, but my confidence is probably 70%. Then if you are not sure, you could then send the picture in the diagnosis by a click of a button to the consultant.'

A confidence level is not a guarantee. A model can be fairly sure and still wrong, especially when it sees something it was never trained on.

Step 3 of 5Your call: act on it, or ask an expert?

The app keeps people in the loop. Three experts in crop disease agreed to have their phone numbers in the app, and with one button a farmer can send the diagnosis to one of them, who confirms it or says it is wrong.

Example data

Farmers without a smartphone, or stuck with the app, can go to an e-kiosk attendant in their community, who helps them diagnose the problem and teaches families to use their phones.

Step 4 of 5What the app recommends, and in which language

Farmers had seen chemical sprays harm the soil they depend on for future harvests, so the app recommends environmentally friendly remedies. When we spoke with the team, they gave the example of fall armyworm on maize, one of the crops in the first version: farmers were shown how to make a spray from neem leaves. That is the team's example, not advice from us.

Language shaped the design too. The diagnosis comes back in Twi, one of the most widely spoken languages in Ghana. The recommendations are read aloud, but in English, because text to speech for Twi was not available. The app works in a mix of the two until it is.

Twi on this page: to confirm with the team

We have not written any Twi here. Step titles or a sample diagnosis in Twi would come from the team or a Twi speaker they trust.

Step 5 of 5When the rains stopped

The first version covered four crops: cassava, cashew, maize and tomato. It worked, until a year when the weather turned.

'That particular year, what you realize is that even if you use the app, you see that it will identify every crop as sick.'

A model trained on leaves from normal seasons read drought as disease, like the third example leaf in this walk-through. The team made a hard decision: they dropped three of the four crops and focused on cashew, which has deep roots, grows in every season and is now the main cash crop in the area. The lesson they drew is that a model cannot rely on historical patterns alone; data collection has to keep going as the climate changes.

The ecological reading

The ecological choices run through the whole project: remedies that protect the soil, and a tool that had to adapt to a changing climate. The team also thought about the footprint of building the model. Their local graphics card had eight gigabytes of memory and the local power supply was inefficient, so they paid for Google Colab and trained there, partly to lower the carbon footprint. That is their reasoning; how much it saved was not measured.

To think about: when should a tool hand over to a person, and who should that person be?
