PetaBencana Frugal

Report a flood, then see it on the map

PetaBencana uses software for what computers do well, finding flood posts and replying at scale, and asks people standing in the water to say what is happening, in the terms they already use.

PetaBencana runs public disaster maps in Indonesia and, as MapaKalamidad, in the Philippines. Instead of trying to predict floods, the team asks people where it floods now. In five short steps, follow one report from a social media post to the public map.

Example data The posts, the city, the reports and the map below are examples we wrote and drew. They are not PetaBencana's screens or data. Nothing you choose or type leaves your browser.

Step 1 of 5Which posts are about a flood happening now?

When it rains hard, people post about it. PetaBencana's system looks for posts about disasters using language and location. Read these five example posts and decide which ones describe a flood happening now.

Example data

    Software that reads posts at this speed will get some wrong, especially warnings, jokes and memories. That is why the next step asks a person.

    Step 2 of 5An automated reply asks the person

    When a post looks like a flood report, the system replies automatically. It asks whether the person is really in a flood and, if they are, sends a link to a report form. Each link is for one report.

    'When we experimented with deploying an automated reply for the first time, asking people if they were really in a flood, we got thousands of reports in a few hours.'

    How deep is the water?

    People describe a flood by their own body: up to the ankle, the knee or the waist. PetaBencana's report uses a simple visual slider with a drawing of a person and a car wheel, and turns the answer into centimeters.

    What the report does not ask for: your name, your account handle or anything else about you. PetaBencana keeps reports anonymous and stores no personal information. The real form also takes one optional photo. Ours does not, because this page collects nothing.

    The frugal reading

    PetaBencana chose not to build a model that predicts floods. Floods are shaped by human decisions and failing infrastructure as much as by rain, so the team built on what people already do in a flood: tell each other what is happening. The software finds the posts and sends the replies; people supply the knowledge; the result is open to everyone.

    The team also argues that bigger is not better for this kind of work:

    'Besides being far more energy efficient and better for the environment, smaller better-curated datasets are also much more accurate than large models.'

    PetaBencana, from our case study Quote check pending

    We are preparing an experiment that puts this claim to a simple test: large or small model, what does each cost?

    To think about: in your own work, where are people already sharing the information you need, and what would it take to ask them directly?
